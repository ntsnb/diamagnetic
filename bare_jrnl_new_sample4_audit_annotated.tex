\begin{abstract}
This paper studies online multivariate regional load forecasting under \auditbad{delayed feedback}{P03} and distribution shift. We develop a plug-and-play forecasting framework that combines invariant representation learning, factor-based expert modeling, and a three-stage online controller. The framework separates stable predictive structure from variant fluctuations, \auditbad{performs relevance-guided sparse updates only when experts become unstable}{P13}, and applies transient drift compensation at inference time. Extensive experiments on multiple load forecasting datasets and multiple transformer-based backbones show that the proposed framework \auditbad{consistently improves over frozen deployment and representative online baselines}{P04} under shifted online streams.\auditnote{P04}{需改为证据范围内的表述：相对 Frozen 有增益、对在线基线为 competitive，不能写成普遍超越。}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Regional load forecasting in real deployment environments is inherently nonstationary. Seasonal transitions, weather fluctuations, and changing demand patterns cause the online test stream to deviate from the distribution seen during offline training. In such settings, a strong offline forecaster alone is often insufficient, while naive online fine-tuning can be unstable under \auditbad{delayed labels}{P03} and concept drift. This motivates an online forecasting framework that can preserve stable mechanisms, localize unstable components, and adapt conservatively when the distribution shifts.

The method developed in this paper follows that principle. We first isolate invariant predictive structure through environment-aware representation learning, then model dynamic load-generation mechanisms through a factorized multi-expert forecasting backbone, and finally perform structured online adaptation through Stage~I, Stage~II, and Stage~III. Stage~I recalibrates the factor mixture under \auditbad{delayed labels}{P03}, Stage~II \auditbad{performs relevance-guided sparse expert updates when instability is detected}{P13}, and Stage~III applies transient correction based on the discrepancy between variant and invariant representations.

Unlike methods tied to a single predictor, the proposed design is formulated as a plug-and-play framework. The same online controller can be combined with \auditbad{multiple transformer-based backbones}{P23}, which allows the experiments to evaluate both forecasting strength and backbone generality. The following sections present the problem formulation, the method, and the empirical validation.

\section{Preliminaries}
\subsection{Notation}
Let $\{\mathbf{x}_t\}_{t=1}^{T}$ denote a multivariate time series, where $\mathbf{x}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{N}$ and $N$ is the number of observed variables. We formulate time-series forecasting via a sliding-window scheme: for each reference time $t$, an input lookback window of length $L$ is used to predict an output horizon window of length $H$. Specifically, we define the input window $\mathbf{X}_t \triangleq [\mathbf{x}_{t-L+1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_t] \in \mathbb{R}^{L \times N}$ and the output window $\mathbf{Y}_t \triangleq [y_{t+1},\ldots,y_{t+H}] \in \mathbb{R}^{H}$, where $y_t$ denotes the target variable. The formulation extends directly to multivariate targets by letting $\mathbf{Y}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times d_{\mathrm{out}}}$.

A forecasting model $f_{\theta}: \mathbb{R}^{L \times N} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^{H}$ parameterized by $\theta$ maps past observations to future targets, and $\widehat{\mathbf{Y}}_t = f_{\theta}(\mathbf{X}_t)$. Given a pointwise loss function $\ell(\widehat{\mathbf{Y}}_t,\mathbf{Y}_t)$, standard training minimizes the empirical risk over the training set\auditnote{P08}{此处集合上标仍用 N，但前文 N 已定义为变量数；训练样本数建议改为 M 或 $T_s$。} $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tr}}=\{(\mathbf{X}_t,\mathbf{Y}_t)\}_{t=1}^{N}$, formulated as:  $\min_{\theta}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^{N}\ell\big(f_{\theta}(\mathbf{X}_t),\mathbf{Y}_t\big)$.

\subsection{Online Learning in OOD Settings}
Let $P_{\mathrm{tr}}(X,Y)$ and $P_{\mathrm{te}}(X,Y)$ denote the joint distributions of input windows $X$ and targets $Y$ for training and test. OOD forecasting has $P_{\mathrm{te}}(X,Y)\neq P_{\mathrm{tr}}(X,Y)$ and can be characterized by the following shifts.

\textbf{Covariate shift.} $P_{\mathrm{te}}(X)\neq P_{\mathrm{tr}}(X)$; the input marginal changes, e.g., meteorological covariates differ across years or net-load profiles shift after large-scale renewable integration.

\textbf{Label shift.} $P_{\mathrm{te}}(Y)\neq P_{\mathrm{tr}}(Y)$; the target marginal changes, e.g., electrification increases mean load or industrial expansion alters peak frequency, causing bias under historical target density.

\textbf{Conditional shift.} $P_{\mathrm{te}}(Y|X)\neq P_{\mathrm{tr}}(Y|X)$; the mapping from history to future changes due to altered influence mechanisms among variables, e.g., demand response changes load sensitivity to temperature.

\textbf{Latent shift induced by unobserved variables.} Let $Z$ denote unobserved drivers affecting both $X$ and $Y$. Changes in $P_{\mathrm{te}}(Z)\neq P_{\mathrm{tr}}(Z)$ can induce joint shifts in observed variables. In load forecasting, such shifts may be triggered by extreme weather or public health emergencies, e.g., sudden demand changes beyond recorded covariates.

In practice, multiple shift types can co-occur, complicating OOD generalization. This motivates online learning in OOD settings to mitigate distribution drift by continually transforming newly observed series into training samples and updating model parameters to minimize forecast errors on recent data. However, conventional online learning faces three structural constraints: (i) due to the feedback delay in \auditbad{Section~1}{P08}, updates can only use labels arriving at least \auditbad{$L$ steps later}{P08}; (ii) the online phase may encounter novel operating regimes outside the historical distribution. (iii) some distribution drift is caused by variant unobserved factors. If updates are not applied selectively, the model can overfit and experience sustained performance degradation after the drift subsides. 

Therefore, a proactive online adaptation framework is needed to (i) enable rapid parameter adjustment under drift, and (ii) explicitly classify stable, long-term changes from fluctuations driven by variant unobserved factors, so that different online learning strategies can be applied accordingly.

\subsection{Structural Causal Model}

To provide a principled basis for such selective adaptation, \auditbad{Structural Causal Model (SCM) is introduced to represent latent drivers and causal mechanisms, providing a principled basis for reasoning}{P10}. SCM consists of endogenous variables $V$, exogenous variables $U$, structural assignments $V_i \leftarrow f_i(\mathrm{Pa}(V_i), U_i)$ for each $V_i \in V$, and a joint distribution $P(U)$ over the exogenous variables. To separate long-term changes from variant fluctuations, an invariant learning module is introduced to extract features $\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{I}}$ and a sufficiently predictable component $\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{suf}}$ whose conditional relation $P_e(\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{suf}}|\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{I}})$ is invariant across environments (Fig.~1(b)). $\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{I}}$ is the subset of inputs $\mathbf{X}$ that provides sufficient, environment-stable predictive information for $\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{suf}}$, and both are intended to exclude variant effects from unobserved factors. The following \auditbad{invariance assumption}{P11} is adopted:

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{Y}=\alpha(Z)\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{suf}}+\beta(Z) \mathbf{1}.
\end{equation}
Here $\alpha(Z)$ and $\beta(Z)$ are mapping functions of $Z$. The effect of $Z$ is mitigated via Instance Residual Normalization (IRN):
\begin{equation}
\tilde{\mathrm{Res}}_t=\frac{Y_t-\mu(Y_t)}{\sigma(Y_t)}-\frac{\widehat{Y}_t-\mu(\widehat{Y}_t)}{\sigma(\widehat{Y}_t)},
\end{equation}
where $\mu(\cdot)$ and $\sigma(\cdot)$ denote the mean and standard deviation. \auditbad{IRN ensures the residuals have mean 0 and variance $2-2\mathrm{cov}(\widehat{Y},Y)$}{P11}, where $\mathrm{cov}$ denotes the covariance. This yields the surrogate loss
\begin{equation}
\ell(\widehat{Y},Y)=\mathrm{MSE}(\tilde{\mathrm{Res}},0).
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}

\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}

\begin{equation}
\min_{\rho,\phi}\ \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{TIL}}

\end{equation}

This design enables controlled comparison of how variant fluctuations influence embeddings and provides a basis for subsequent online adaptation strategies that explicitly target variant effects.

\subsection{\auditbad{C}{P07}}
In real-world load forecasting, observed covariates $\mathbf{x}_t$ (e.g., temperature, humidity, wind speed) rarely cause the load $\mathbf{Y}_t$ directly. Instead, $\mathbf{x}_t$ is assumed to act through latent factor states $\mathbf{F}_t$, each representing an unobserved but physically meaningful driver (e.g., air-conditioning usage intensity or industrial activity). These factors are causally independent, respond to exogenous conditions $\mathbf{x}_t$, and contribute to $\mathbf{Y}_t$ in parallel. The goal is to disentangle factor-specific mechanisms and their time-varying contributions rather than attributing $\mathbf{Y}_t$ directly to $\mathbf{x}_t$. 

Inspired by the conditional-mixture view of dynamic mechanisms, a dynamic graph is modeled as a conditionally weighted superposition of multiple static graphs. Each static graph encodes a distinct, potentially nonlinear interaction pattern, and the weights vary with system state, yielding a factor-model view of dynamic causality.

Consider a multivariate time series $\{\mathbf{x}_t\}_{t=1}^{T}$ with $\mathbf{x}_t\in\mathbb{R}^{N}$. We represent the target $\mathbf{Y}_t$ as a weighted combination of $n_k$ factor generators:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{Y}_t&=\boldsymbol{\alpha}(t)\odot^{\dagger}\mathbf{f}_{\phi}\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{t-L:t}\right)\\
&=\sum_{k=1}^{n_k}\alpha_{k}(t)\,f_{\phi_k}\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{t-L:t}\right),
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $f_{\phi_k}$ is the $k$-th factor generator and $\alpha_{k}(t)$ is its coefficient during time window $t-L:t$. Factor coefficients $\boldsymbol{\alpha}(t)$ are obtained through a feature mapping of $\mathbf{z}_{t}$, and they are both assumed stable within the time window:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{h}_{t}&=m_{\psi}\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{t-L:t}\right),\\
\mathbf{z}_{t}&=m_{\theta}\!\left(\mathbf{h}_{t}\right),\\
\boldsymbol{\alpha}(t)&=m_{\eta}\!\left(\mathbf{z}_{t}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{n_k},
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Here $m_{\psi}$ is a temporal Transformer encoder with pooling that yields the summary embedding $\mathbf{h}_t$, $m_{\theta}$ is a variational encoder that models factor features $\mathbf{z}_t$ with mean and variance predicted from $\mathbf{h}_t$, and $m_{\eta}$ maps $\mathbf{z}_t$ to coefficients $\boldsymbol{\alpha}(t)$. Substituting these components yields the forecasting form:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{Y}_t&=m_{\eta}\left(m_{\theta}\left(m_{\psi}\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{t-L:t}\right)\right)\right)\odot^{\dagger}\mathbf{f}_{\phi}\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{t-L:t}\right)
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

We next detail the temporal encoder $m_{\psi}$, which produces timewise representations via standard self-attention blocks along the time axis. The input is first embedded as $\mathbf{H}_t^{(0)}=\mathbf{Embedding}(\mathbf{X}_t)\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d_h}$. For each Transformer block, the computation is:
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{H}_t^{(l)}\mathbf{W}_Q,\quad \mathbf{K}=\mathbf{H}_t^{(l)}\mathbf{W}_K,\quad \mathbf{V}=\mathbf{H}_t^{(l)}\mathbf{W}_V,\\
\mathrm{Attn}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{K},\mathbf{V})=\mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_h}}\right)\mathbf{V},\\
\mathbf{A}^{(l)}=\mathrm{MHA}(\mathbf{H}_t^{(l)})\mathbf{W}_O,\\
\widetilde{\mathbf{H}}_t^{(l)}=\mathrm{LN}\!\left(\mathbf{H}_t^{(l)}+\mathbf{A}^{(l)}\right),\\
\mathbf{H}_t^{(l+1)}=\mathrm{LN}\!\left(\widetilde{\mathbf{H}}_t^{(l)}+\mathrm{FFN}\!\left(\widetilde{\mathbf{H}}_t^{(l)}\right)\right).
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{W}_Q,\mathbf{W}_K,\mathbf{W}_V\in\mathbb{R}^{d_h\times d_{(QKV)}}$, and the corresponding global summary embedding $\mathbf{h}_t\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{h}}}$ is obtained from the final layer output $\mathbf{H}_t^{(F)}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d_h}$ as $\mathbf{h}_t=\mathrm{MLP}_{h}\!\left(\mathbf{H}_t^{(F)}\right)$.

Given the window representation, a feature vector $\mathbf{z}_t\in\mathbb{R}^{n_k d_b}$ with factor blocks $\mathbf{z}_t=[\mathbf{z}^{(1)}_t;\dots;\mathbf{z}^{(n_k)}_t]$ is introduced, where $\mathbf{z}^{(m)}_t\in\mathbb{R}^{d_b}$ denotes the latent feature associated with the $m$-th factor mechanism and $d_b$ is a shared hyperparameter that sets the dimensions for all factor features. The feature vector $\mathbf{z}_t$ is inferred by a variational encoder with prior distribution $p(\mathbf{z}_t)$ defined as a standard Gaussian and variational posterior $q_{\zeta}(\mathbf{z}_t\mid \mathbf{X}_t)$ parameterized by encoder-predicted mean and diagonal variance:
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
\mathbf{z}_t\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}),\\
q_{\zeta }(\mathbf{z}_t\mid\mathbf{X}_t)=\mathcal{N}\!\big(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\zeta}(\mathbf{h}_t),\mathrm{diag}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^2_{\zeta}(\mathbf{h}_t))\big).
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}

The decoder reconstructs the window via a feedforward network, $\widehat{\mathbf{X}}_t=\mathrm{Dec}_x(\mathbf{z}_t)$. We then map the latent features to factor weights, assumed stable within the window: $\boldsymbol{\alpha}(t)=m_{\eta}(\mathbf{z}_t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n_k}$.

For factor-wise prediction, a sparse mixture-of-experts (MoE) separates the modeling of distinct factors. Each expert corresponds to $f_{\phi_k}$ and is implemented as a Transformer block dedicated to the $k$-th factor; unlike the temporal encoder, these blocks employ multivariate self-attention along the variable axis and yield embeddings $\mathbf{e}^{(m)}_{t,\ell}$. The expert embeddings are first fused with $\boldsymbol{\alpha}(t)$ and then mapped to the output:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{e}_{t,\ell}=\sum_{k=1}^{n_k}\alpha_{k}(t)\,\mathbf{e}^{(m)}_{t,\ell},\qquad
\widehat{\mathbf{y}}_{t,\ell}=f_o(\mathbf{e}_{t,\ell}).
\end{equation}

To ensure that experts encode distinct and stable mechanisms rather than redundant correlations, we introduce a relevance-based regularizer. Following layer-wise relevance propagation (LRP), the prediction is decomposed into node-level relevance scores that are propagated backward through the network. Specifically,
\begin{equation}
\sum_{d\in V_L}R_d^{(L)}=\cdots=\sum_{d\in V_l}R_d^{(l)}=\cdots=\sum_{d\in V_1}R_d^{(1)}.
\end{equation}
where $V_l$ denotes the set of nodes in layer $l$, and $R_d^{(l)}$ denotes the relevance score of node $d\in V_l$, namely, its contribution to the final prediction at layer $l$. This equality explicitly describes the cross-layer contribution relation: the total relevance is conserved when propagated from deep to shallow layers.
For the transition from layer $l+1$ to $l$, relevance is redistributed as:
\begin{equation}
R_i^{(l)}=\sum_{j\in V_{l+1}}\frac{a_i^{(l)}w_{ij}}{\sum_{i'\in V_l}a_{i'}^{(l)}w_{i'j}+b_j}\,R_j^{(l+1)},
\end{equation}
which further implies the local conservation relation $\sum_{i\in V_l}R_{i\leftarrow j}^{(l)}=R_j^{(l+1)}$, $\forall\, j\in V_{l+1}$. Here, $a_i^{(l)}$ is the activation of node $i$ in layer $l$, $w_{ij}$ is the weight from node $i$ to node $j$, and $b_j$ is the bias term of node $j$. Larger positive relevance indicates stronger causal contribution.

To quantify differences in the mechanisms captured by factor experts, we define an expert-wise discrepancy using relevance maps. For a target variable $i$, each expert yields a two-dimensional LRP relevance map over the window $t\!-\!L:t$, denoted by $\mathbf{R}_{i,t}^{(m)}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times L}$, $m=1,\ldots,n_k$, with entries:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{R}_{i,t}^{(m)}[v,\tau]=\left|R_{d(v,\tau)}^{(1,m\rightarrow i,t)}\right|,\quad v=1,\ldots,N,\ \tau=1,\ldots,L.
\end{equation}
where $R_{d(v,\tau)}^{(1,m\rightarrow i,t)}$ is the input-layer relevance for variable $v$ at lag $\tau$ when expert $m$ explains target $i$ at time $t$, and $|\cdot|$ keeps magnitude. We treat $\mathbf{R}_{i,t}^{(m)}$ as an $N\times L$ relevance surface and compute discrepancy per variable along the temporal axis. For variable $v\in\{1,\ldots,N\}$, let $\mathbf{r}_{i,v,t}^{(m)}=\mathbf{R}_{i,t}^{(m)}[v,:]\in\mathbb{R}^{L}$.
To jointly capture temporal-shape mismatch and contribution-magnitude mismatch, a composite discrepancy is defined. First, to quantify contribution magnitude, define the total positive mass:
\begin{equation}
M_{i,v}^{(m)}(t)=\sum_{\tau=1}^{L}\mathbf{r}_{i,v,t}^{(m)}(\tau),
\end{equation}
Then, to quantify temporal-shape mismatch, each profile $\mathbf{r}_{i,v,t}^{(m)}$ is normalized into a lag distribution:
\begin{equation}
\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_{i,v,t}^{(m)}(\tau)=\frac{\mathbf{r}_{i,v,t}^{(m)}(\tau)}{M_{i,v}^{(m)}(t)+\delta},\quad \tau=1,\ldots,L.
\end{equation}
Here, $\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_{i,v,t}^{(m)}$ is the lag distribution of expert $m$ for variable $v$ when explaining target $i$ at time $t$.
For two experts indexed by $p$ and $q$, the shape discrepancy on variable $v$ is:
\begin{equation}
\Delta_{i,v}^{\mathrm{shape}}(p,q;t)=W_1\!\left(\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_{i,v,t}^{(p)},\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_{i,v,t}^{(q)}\right),
\end{equation}
where $W_1(\cdot,\cdot)$ denotes the Wasserstein Distance between two discrete lag distributions. In parallel, the magnitude discrepancy is defined as:
\begin{equation}
\Delta_{i,v}^{\mathrm{mass}}(p,q;t)=\left|M_{i,v}^{(p)}(t)-M_{i,v}^{(q)}(t)\right|.
\end{equation}
Accordingly, the variable-level discrepancy $\Delta_{i,v}^{(p,q)}$ is defined by combining the shape and magnitude terms:
\begin{equation}
\Delta_{i,v}^{(p,q)}(t)=\Delta_{i,v}^{\mathrm{shape}}(p,q;t)+\lambda_{\mathrm{mass}}\Delta_{i,v}^{\mathrm{mass}}(p,q;t),
\end{equation}
where $\lambda_{\mathrm{mass}}$ is the hyperparameter that balances shape mismatch and magnitude mismatch. Finally, the overall discrepancy $\mathcal{D}_{i}(t)$ is obtained by aggregating over variables and expert pairs:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{D}_{i}(t)=\sum_{v=1}^{N}\sum_{1\le p<q\le n_k}\Delta_{i,v}^{(p,q)}(t).
\end{equation}
The overall objective function $\mathcal{L}(t)$ consists of the supervised prediction loss, the VAE reconstruction (ELBO) loss, and the relevance-based regularizer. Specifically,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}(t)=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pred}}(t)+\lambda_{1}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{VAE}}(t)+\lambda_{2}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathcal{D}_{i}(t),
\end{equation}
where $\lambda_{1}$ and $\lambda_{2}$ weight the VAE loss and the relevance-based regularizer, respectively. The supervised prediction loss over the window is:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pred}}(t)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\ell\!\left(\mathbf{y}_{t-L+\ell},\widehat{\mathbf{y}}_{t,\ell}\right).
\end{equation}
The VAE objective is composed of a reconstruction term given by the negative log-likelihood and a KL divergence regularizer:
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{VAE}}(t)=\mathbb{E}_{q_{\zeta}}\!\left[-\log p_{\theta}(\mathbf{X}_t\mid\mathbf{z}_t)\right]\\
+\beta D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\zeta}(\mathbf{h}_t),\mathrm{diag}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^2_{\zeta}(\mathbf{h}_t)))\,\|\,\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})\right).
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}

\subsection{\auditbad{D}{P07}}
We now present the \auditbad{two-stage online adaptation}{P05} that operates on top of the factor-mechanism backbone.
\textbf{Stage I (weight-only adaptation under stable mechanisms).}
We introduce a stability metric to quantify whether each expert's causal mechanism remains consistent over short horizons. Using the previously defined LRP relevance map $\mathbf{R}_{i,t}^{(m)}$, we normalize it as $\hat{\mathbf{R}}_{i,t}^{(m)}=\mathbf{R}_{i,t}^{(m)}/\|\mathbf{R}_{i,t}^{(m)}\|_{1}$ and define a temporal stability score
\begin{equation}
S_{i}^{(m)}(t)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{W}|}\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{W}} \cos\!\left(\hat{\mathbf{R}}_{i,t}^{(m)},\hat{\mathbf{R}}_{i,t-\tau}^{(m)}\right),
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{W}$ is a short rolling window. This score measures the consistency of relevance distributions over time. An expert is considered stable if $S_{i}^{(m)}(t)\ge\gamma$, and Stage~I is activated for stable experts.

With stable mechanisms identified, we focus on the remaining short-term variability in how factor features are combined. In this stage, each expert's internal parameters are kept fixed and online adaptation updates only the combination weights. As defined earlier, the factor coefficients are produced from the VAE latent feature vector $\mathbf{z}_t$ through a lightweight feed-forward network $m_{\eta}$, yielding dynamic weights $\boldsymbol{\alpha}(t)=m_{\eta}(\mathbf{z}_t)$.
The design supports proactive adaptation before labels arrive because the combination weights are generated in real time from $\mathbf{z}_t$. By default, the parameters of the VAE and each expert remain frozen and only $m_{\eta}$ is updated online when delayed labels become available to better recover factor weights from $\mathbf{z}_t$.

 \textbf{Stage II (mechanism-aware adaptation for unstable experts).}
LRP relevance maps are jointly influenced by the input sample $\mathbf{X}_t$, the model parameters $\theta$, and the factor mechanisms instantiated by each expert. Hence, attributing relevance drift solely to mechanism change is confounded by both parameter evolution and input distribution shift. Stage~II addresses this by (i) stabilizing relevance maps with a slow-moving reference model and (ii) aligning LRP drift to parameter updates via a low-rank adaptation generator while restricting updates to unstable experts and their LRP-localized parameter subsets.

We maintain a reference model with the same architecture but slower weight dynamics, updated by an exponential moving average every $K$ optimization steps:
\begin{equation}
\theta_{\mathrm{ref}} \leftarrow (1-\eta)\theta_{\mathrm{ref}} + \eta\,\theta,\qquad \eta\in[0,1).
\end{equation}
A scheduled $\eta$ balances early adaptation and long-term stability, allowing $\theta_{\mathrm{ref}}$ to provide stable relevance signals while tracking gradual mechanism evolution.

For the same input window $\mathbf{X}_t$, we compute LRP maps $\mathbf{R}_{i,t}^{(m)}(\theta)$ and $\mathbf{R}_{i,t}^{(m)}(\theta_{\mathrm{ref}})$ from the current and reference models. Their difference isolates mechanism/parameter discrepancies while suppressing confounding by input shift:
\begin{equation}
\Delta \mathbf{R}_{i,t}^{(m)} = \mathbf{R}_{i,t}^{(m)}(\theta)-\mathbf{R}_{i,t}^{(m)}(\theta_{\mathrm{ref}}).
\end{equation}

Let the unstable expert set be $\mathcal{U}_t=\{m \mid S_i^{(m)}(t) < \gamma\}$, where $S_i^{(m)}(t)$ is the stability score from Stage~I. Stage~II updates only the parameters associated with experts in $\mathcal{U}_t$.

For each unstable expert, we first identify input-layer relevance locations with the largest discrepancies, then back-trace these locations to locate the most influential neurons in each layer. The input-layer relevance map is indexed by variable--lag pairs $(v,\tau)$; each location corresponds to node $d(v,\tau)$. Let $\Omega_t=\mathrm{TopK}(|\Delta \mathbf{R}^{(1)}_t|,k_{\Omega})$ denote the set of variable--lag locations with the largest discrepancy. We propagate relevance from $\Omega_t$ to each layer and select the neurons that contribute most to those locations. At layer $l$, we define:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{S}_t^{(l)}=\mathrm{TopK}\Big(\big|\Delta \mathbf{R}^{(l)}_{t,\Omega}\big|,k\Big),\qquad
\mathcal{S}_t=\bigcup_l \mathcal{S}_t^{(l)}.
\end{equation}
Only parameters in $\mathcal{S}_t$ (restricted to experts in $\mathcal{U}_t$) are adapted.

We embed the relevance maps using lightweight LRP encoders:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{h}_{t}=\mathrm{Enc}_{\mathrm{LRP}}\!\left(\mathbf{R}_{i,t}^{(m)}(\theta)\right),\quad
\mathbf{h}^{\mathrm{ref}}_{t}=\mathrm{Enc}_{\mathrm{LRP}}\!\left(\mathbf{R}_{i,t}^{(m)}(\theta_{\mathrm{ref}})\right),
\end{equation}
and define the drift representation $\mathbf{d}_t=\mathbf{h}_{t}-\mathbf{h}^{\mathrm{ref}}_{t}$.

To align LRP drift with parameter changes, we use a \auditbad{parameter-efficient adaptation generator}{P15}. For simplicity, consider a linear transformation in the $l$-th layer with parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(l)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{in}}\times d_{\mathrm{out}}}$. Given a small bottleneck $r\ll\min(d_{\mathrm{in}},d_{\mathrm{out}})$, we compute adaptation coefficients by
\begin{equation}
\left[{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{(l)}_t,{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(l)}_t\right]
=\mathbf{W}^{(l)}_2\,\sigma\!\left(\mathbf{W}^{(l)}_1\,\mathbf{d}_t+\mathbf{b}^{(l)}\right)+\mathbf{1},
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{W}^{(l)}_1\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d_c}$, $\mathbf{W}^{(l)}_2\in\mathbb{R}^{(d_{\mathrm{in}}+d_{\mathrm{out}})\times r}$, $\mathbf{b}^{(l)}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}$, $d_c$ is the drift-embedding dimension, and $\sigma(\cdot)$ is the sigmoid. We use layer-wise bias terms $\mathbf{b}^{(l)}$ while sharing $\mathbf{W}_1$ and $\mathbf{W}_2$ across layers of the same type to reduce overhead.

We then construct a binary mask $\mathbf{M}_t^{(l)}\in\{0,1\}^{d_{\mathrm{in}}\times d_{\mathrm{out}}}$ from $\mathcal{S}_t^{(l)}$ and allow updates only on those entries. The adapted parameters are
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(l)}_t=\left(\mathbf{M}_t^{(l)}\odot {\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{(l)}_t {\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(l)\top}_t\right)\odot \boldsymbol{\theta}_t^{(l)}.
\end{equation}
Equivalently, the parameter adjustment is $\Delta\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(l)}_t=\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(l)}_t-\boldsymbol{\theta}_t^{(l)}$, which modifies only masked entries. This ensures that Stage~II adapts only LRP-localized parameters of unstable experts, improving stability in online updates. 

\textbf{Stage III.} To further enhance adaptation to variant fluctuations, a parallel mechanism is introduced that operates on the embeddings produced by the invariant and variant branches of the invariant-learning module. The same \auditbad{parameter-efficient generator is applied to both embeddings}{P15}, and the resulting parameter shifts are \auditbad{optimized using the post-update prediction error}{P13}.

Using the invariant and variant embeddings $\phi_{\mathrm{I}}(X_t)$ and $\phi_{\mathrm{V}}(X_t)$ at time $t$, the concept drift between the two branches is computed as their difference:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{d}^{\mathrm{V}}_t=\phi_{\mathrm{V}}(X_t)-\phi_{\mathrm{I}}(X_t).
\end{equation}
This definition aligns with the role of the variant branch stated earlier: it preserves variation relative to the invariant branch and provides a reference for quantifying variant effects in the embedding space. The subsequent parameter update follows the same \auditbad{parameter-efficient adaptation generator as in Stage~II}{P15}, but uses $\mathbf{d}^{\mathrm{V}}_t$ to target variant-induced deviations.

For a linear layer $\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(l)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{in}}\times d_{\mathrm{out}}}$, the shared low-rank generator produces adaptation coefficients from each drift,
\begin{equation}
\left[\tilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{(l)}_t,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(l)}_t\right]
=\tilde{\mathbf{W}}^{(l)}_2\,\sigma\!\left(\tilde{\mathbf{W}}^{(l)}_1\,\mathbf{d}^{\mathrm{V}}_t+\tilde{\mathbf{b}}^{(l)}\right)+\mathbf{1},
\end{equation}
and the adapted parameters used for forecasting at time $t$ are
\begin{equation}
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{(l)}_t=\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{(l)}_t\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(l)\top}_t\right)\odot \widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(l)}_t,
\end{equation}
where $\odot$ denotes the element-wise product. The induced adjustment is
\begin{equation}
\Delta\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(l)}_t=\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{(l)}_t\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(l)\top}_t-\mathbf{1}\right)\odot \widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(l)}_t.
\end{equation}
The \auditbad{generator parameters are then updated using the prediction error of the adapted model}{P15}, which strengthens its ability to proactively adjust parameters under variant fluctuations. 

Here $\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(l)}_t$ denotes the parameters after Stage~II at time $t$. Stage~III produces a temporary update on top of these Stage~II parameters for the current step only. The fluctuation update $\Delta\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(l)}_t=\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(l)}_t-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(l)}_t$ is applied only for prediction at time $t$ and is discarded afterward. At time $t+1$, a new fluctuation update is computed on top of the updated Stage~II parameters, so transient adaptations do not accumulate across steps.

\subsection{\auditbad{E}{P07}}
We evaluate the proposed framework under both offline forecasting and online adaptation settings. The empirical study is organized around three questions: 1) whether the proposed factor-mechanism architecture provides a strong static forecasting backbone; 2) whether each online stage improves over a frozen deployment baseline under \auditbad{delayed feedback}{P03}; and 3) whether combining Stage~I, Stage~II, and Stage~III yields the \auditbad{strongest overall online performance}{P25}.

\subsubsection{Datasets}
To align the experiments with the regional load forecasting objective of this paper, the main benchmarks are three multivariate load datasets with exogenous covariates: \textit{Panama}, \textit{Tetouan}, and \textit{Spain Energy--Weather}. The \textit{Panama} dataset contains the national electricity demand together with meteorological variables from multiple cities and calendar indicators. The \textit{Tetouan} dataset contains zonal power consumption together with local weather and solar-flow variables. The \textit{Spain Energy--Weather} dataset contains hourly system load together with weather and degree-day features. In our experiments, the prediction targets are \texttt{nat\_demand} for \textit{Panama}, \texttt{Zone 1 Power Consumption} for \textit{Tetouan}, and \texttt{actual\_load} for \textit{Spain Energy--Weather}. All datasets are processed in chronological order. \auditbad{No random shuffling is applied across the temporal axis}{P17} so as to preserve causal ordering and maintain consistency with the online adaptation scenario.

In addition to these public benchmarks, we further curate a new \textit{Lishui-9} load dataset family from operational records of nine county-level regions in Lishui, Zhejiang Province. The family contains nine county-level multivariate load forecasting tasks with matched meteorological covariates and serves as an additional real-world validation set for all key experimental modules.

For each dataset, the samples are first split chronologically into training and validation subsets with ratios of 55\% and 10\%, respectively. The remaining suffix is reserved for online evaluation under a \auditbad{shifted-stream protocol}{P18} described later. This preserves chronological causality while ensuring that the online deployment stream differs from the offline training regime.

\subsubsection{Data Processing}
Given a multivariate time series with $N$ variables, sliding-window samples are constructed with a lookback length $L=24$ and a forecasting horizon $H=12$. For each timestamp $t$, the model takes the historical window $\mathbf{X}_{t-L+1:t}\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times N}$ as input and predicts the target trajectory over the next $H$ steps. All variables are standardized using only the statistics computed from the training split, and the same normalization parameters are then applied to the validation and test splits to avoid information leakage.

Standard cleaning and interpolation are applied when needed before sliding-window construction.

\subsubsection{Online Evaluation Protocol}
The online stage is evaluated on the chronologically ordered test stream. Labels arrive with \auditbad{delayed feedback}{P03}, and therefore parameter updates are only triggered after the corresponding delayed target becomes available.\auditnote{P13}{代码审计显示 Stage II/Full 接受门存在当前标签使用风险，需修协议或标成 oracle-assisted。} We compare the following variants:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Baseline}: the offline model is frozen and directly applied to the online stream;
\item \textbf{Stage~I}: only the factor-weight predictor is adapted online;
\item \textbf{Stage~II}: only the relevance-guided sparse parameter update is activated for unstable experts;
\item \textbf{Stage~III}: only the transient variant-drift compensation is activated during inference;
\item \textbf{Full Model}: Stage~I, Stage~II, and Stage~III are jointly enabled.
\end{itemize}

Stage~I uses delayed-label supervision together with conservative bias updates, clipping, and rollback protection. \auditbad{Stage~II is triggered only when the relevance-map stability score indicates that an expert becomes unstable}{P13}, and only a small masked subset of expert parameters is allowed to change. Stage~III computes a temporary correction from $\boldsymbol{\phi}_V-\boldsymbol{\phi}_I$ and applies it only to the current prediction step without permanently modifying the model parameters.

\subsubsection{Evaluation Metrics}
For static forecasting, we report Mean Squared Error (MSE) and Mean Absolute Error (MAE) on the validation and test sets. For online adaptation, we further record the real-time prediction error on the test stream and compare each adaptation variant against the frozen baseline. The main criterion in the online setting is whether \auditbad{each individual stage improves over the baseline}{P25} and whether the joint Stage~I+II+III configuration achieves the \auditbad{best overall online forecasting performance}{P25}.

\subsubsection{Distribution-Shifted Online Protocol}
To better reflect the motivation of online learning, we additionally evaluate the strongest backbone under a more challenging shifted-stream protocol. Specifically, after fixing the offline training and validation segments, we \auditbad{search within the later candidate region for a contiguous online test block that maximizes the distribution-shift score}{P18} relative to the training windows. The resulting shift scores are 0.1353 on \textit{Panama}, 0.5036 on \textit{Tetouan}, and 0.5504 on \textit{Spain Energy--Weather}, indicating that the online streams used in this protocol differ \auditbad{substantially}{P20} from the offline training distribution, especially on the latter two datasets.

\subsubsection{Component Ablation}
Beyond the online-stage combinations, we further ablate the key structural components of the framework. The evaluated variants include removing the invariant-learning branch (\textbf{w/o Invariant}), disabling the discrepancy-localized sparse Stage~II update and updating dense layer parameters instead (\textbf{Dense Stage~II}), replacing the VAE gate by a simple MLP factor-weight generator (\textbf{MLP Gate, no VAE}), removing RevIN normalization (\textbf{w/o RevIN}), and collapsing the expert bank into a single expert (\textbf{Single Expert}). These are the common component variants for which we report all four datasets jointly. Controller-only ablations such as \textbf{w/o LRP in Stage~II}, \textbf{w/o EMA Ref}, \textbf{w/o Rollback/Buffer}, and \textbf{Sync All Experts} remain discussed in the text and logs, but are omitted from Table~\ref{tab:ablation_components} because they were not re-run for the full Lishui family under the same protocol. Table~\ref{tab:ablation_components} and Fig.~\ref{fig:ablation_components} therefore focus on the shared ablations that are fully comparable across the four datasets, while Fig.~\ref{fig:lishui_component_county} provides county-level detail for the Lishui family.

The component ablation reveals a clear hierarchy of importance. \auditbad{Removing RevIN or collapsing the multi-expert factor bank causes the largest degradation on both the public suite and the Lishui family}{P26}, which shows that normalized input statistics and mechanism diversity are both critical for robust load forecasting under shift. \auditbad{Removing the invariant branch also degrades the result on most datasets}{P26}, although the magnitude of the drop is smaller than for the normalization and expert-count ablations. Replacing the VAE gate by a simpler MLP gate remains competitive and even improves several datasets, including the Lishui family. This again suggests that the main gains come from the factorized expert framework and the online adaptation policy rather than from one single latent regularization form. We therefore treat the VAE gate as one valid instantiation of the framework rather than the only effective choice.

\subsubsection{t-SNE Analysis of Representation and Controller States}
\auditbad{Following the qualitative protocol used in recent online forecasting studies}{P02}, we further visualize the learned representation spaces by t-SNE. For the three public datasets, Fig.~\ref{fig:tsne_phi_drift} compares the invariant representation $\boldsymbol{\phi}_{I}$ with the Stage~III drift representation $\boldsymbol{\phi}_{V}-\boldsymbol{\phi}_{I}$ for train and online samples, Fig.~\ref{fig:tsne_stagewise} visualizes the Stage~I gate space and the Stage~II relevance-drift controller space, and Fig.~\ref{fig:tsne_stage3_experts} shows the Stage~III gain space and the mechanism space induced by the expert branches. Because \textit{Lishui-9} is a nine-task family rather than a single dataset, we visualize its counties separately in Figs.~\ref{fig:lishui_tsne_drift}--\ref{fig:lishui_tsne_stage3}. Table~\ref{tab:tsne_summary} summarizes the public-dataset indicators, while the Lishui family is analyzed visually at county resolution.

Three observations are consistent across the three datasets. First, the Stage~III drift space is more sensitive to train--online mismatch than the invariant space. The centroid gap in $\boldsymbol{\phi}_{V}-\boldsymbol{\phi}_{I}$ is larger than that in $\boldsymbol{\phi}_{I}$ on all datasets, e.g., $0.7536>0.5424$ on \textit{Panama} and $1.3497>0.5288$ on \textit{Tetouan}. This \auditbad{supports the intended role separation}{P29} of the two branches: the invariant encoder preserves the stable forecasting structure, whereas the variant branch amplifies the distribution shift that should be handled online. Second, the Stage~II controller remains highly sparse. The selected ratio is only $7.50\%$ on \textit{Panama}, $0.63\%$ on \textit{Tetouan}, and $8.75\%$ on \textit{Spain Energy--Weather}, which is consistent with the design of Stage~II as a conservative correction mechanism rather than a dense online finetuning routine. Third, the expert mechanism space exhibits clear inter-expert separation, with mean pairwise centroid distances of $17.32$, $13.64$, and $17.58$ on the three datasets, respectively. This indicates that the expert branches are not collapsing to a single shared solution, but are instead learning distinct factor-specific subspaces.

The Stage~I gate-space visualization also provides a \auditbad{direct interpretation}{P29} of the delayed online recalibration. The train--online gate gap is slightly reduced after Stage~I is enabled on \textit{Panama} and \textit{Tetouan}, while it changes only marginally on \textit{Spain Energy--Weather}. The Stage~III scatter further shows that positive online gains are concentrated in localized drift regions rather than uniformly distributed across all online samples. Taken together, these plots \auditbad{indicate}{P29} that Stage~I mainly performs mild gate re-alignment, Stage~II acts only on a small subset of samples with concentrated relevance-drift signatures, and Stage~III exploits the more clearly shifted drift representation for transient correction.

\subsubsection{Additional Evaluation on the Newly Curated Lishui-9 Dataset Family}
To \auditbad{verify that the framework is not tied to public benchmarks only}{P31}, we additionally evaluate it on the newly curated \textit{Lishui-9} dataset family, which contains nine county-level load forecasting tasks with aligned weather covariates. We first run the same family-level protocol as in the main experiments and average the resulting online error over the nine counties. Table~\ref{tab:lishui_family} shows that the qualitative trend remains \auditbad{consistent}{P31}: Stage~III and Stage~II+III are the strongest family-level variants, while the full Stage~I+II+III controller remains highly competitive. The gain is smaller than that on the three public datasets, which is expected because the county-level tasks are shorter and noisier after alignment, but the direction of the improvement remains stable.

We then perform a dedicated tuned shifted-stream comparison on the same family. Starting from the strongest Lishui-specific Crossformer checkpoint, we compare the frozen model, \textbf{SOLID++}, Ours-Stage~III, and Ours-Full on the shifted online blocks of all nine counties. Table~\ref{tab:lishui_family} shows that the tuned version of the proposed controller narrows the gap to \textbf{SOLID++} \auditbad{substantially}{P31}: the family-level mean online MSE decreases from 0.2265 for the frozen deployment to 0.2254 with Stage~III and to 0.2255 with the full controller, while \textbf{SOLID++} reaches 0.2241. Although the \auditbad{strongest external baseline}{P20} still remains slightly better on this newly curated family, the gap is reduced to approximately \auditbad{$1.3\times 10^{-3}$}{P31}, which is notably smaller than in the initial untuned Lishui runs. This additional result is important for two reasons: it \auditbad{confirms that the framework transfers}{P31} to a newly curated regional load dataset with real alignment noise, and it shows that the main remaining challenge on this family is fine-grained online control rather than the static forecasting backbone itself.

Finally, the county-level views reveal substantial heterogeneity. Figs.~\ref{fig:lishui_shifted_sota_county}--\ref{fig:lishui_component_county} show that some counties benefit most from Stage~III alone, whereas others benefit from Stage~II+III or the full controller. The same phenomenon also appears in the county-level case studies and t-SNE plots in Figs.~\ref{fig:lishui_case_study}--\ref{fig:lishui_tsne_stage3}. Therefore, even on this newly curated dataset family, the core benefits still come from the normalization pipeline and the multi-expert factor mechanism, while the optimal online policy remains county dependent.

\subsubsection{Analysis of Main Results}
\auditbad{The current results support the two main claims of this paper}{P33}. First, the proposed method is a \emph{framework} rather than a model tied to one predictor. After replacing only the temporal encoder, the same invariant-learning module, factor-VAE module, expert mechanism layer, and online adaptation stages \auditbad{remain effective across a broad family of transformer-based forecasting backbones}{P33}. Second, the online gains are not restricted to a single operating point. In the refined experiments, Stage~III \auditbad{consistently improves the frozen baseline}{P33}, while Stage~I and Stage~II provide additional corrections whose effect depends on the interaction between the backbone representation and the online controller.

At the dataset level, the strongest absolute online results are delivered by different backbones on different benchmarks. For iTransformer, the best results are 0.1802 on \textit{Panama}, 0.0829 on \textit{Tetouan}, and 0.2054 on \textit{Spain Energy--Weather}. For FEDformer, the full combination reaches 0.1761 on \textit{Panama}, while Stage~III remains highly competitive on \textit{Tetouan} and \textit{Spain Energy--Weather}. For Crossformer, the full configuration achieves 0.2631 on \textit{Spain Energy--Weather}, which provides the clearest example in the current study that the three online stages can deliver the strongest joint benefit on a tuned backbone. The newly curated \textit{Lishui-9} family complements these public benchmarks: it confirms that the full controller still improves over the frozen family baseline after realistic load--weather alignment and cleaning, and it also reveals that the remaining gap to the \auditbad{strongest external controller}{P20} is now concentrated in the online control policy rather than in the backbone accuracy itself. Overall, the experiments show that the proposed framework can \auditbad{simultaneously provide strong static forecasting accuracy and measurable online adaptation gains}{P33} on multivariate regional load forecasting benchmarks.

\subsubsection{Backbone Generality Discussion}
A key design goal of the proposed method is to decouple the online adaptation mechanism from the specific forecasting backbone. The current results support this design choice. Once the temporal encoder is replaced, the remaining components---the invariant branch, the variant branch, the factor-VAE module, the expert mechanism layer, and the three online stages---can be reused without changing the overall \auditbad{training or deployment protocol}{P34}. From this perspective, the role of the backbone is to provide a strong temporal representation, whereas the proposed framework contributes the mechanism decomposition and the online adaptation logic on top of that representation. The ranking in Table~\ref{tab:backbone_screening} therefore has a clear methodological interpretation: the framework is portable across multiple transformer-based predictors, while the final absolute performance still depends on the representational strength of the chosen backbone.

\subsubsection{Online Adaptation Discussion}
The all-backbone comparison in Table~\ref{tab:all8_online_variants} also clarifies the functional roles of the three online stages. Stage~I adjusts the factor-weight predictor $m_{\eta}$ using delayed labels, and is therefore most useful when the expert mechanisms remain valid but their mixture weights require recalibration. Stage~II performs sparse relevance-guided updates only on unstable experts, so its effect depends more strongly on the quality of the backbone representation and on the reliability of the relevance signal. \auditbad{Stage~III uses the discrepancy}{P29} $\boldsymbol{\phi}_V-\boldsymbol{\phi}_I$ to construct a transient correction at inference time, and is \auditbad{empirically the most stable source of online gain}{P24} in the current benchmark suite. The combined model benefits from these three stages when their respective corrections are complementary rather than redundant, which explains why the strongest joint gains are observed on selected backbone--dataset combinations rather than uniformly across all settings.

\subsubsection{Practical Implications}
From a practical load-forecasting perspective, the current experiments suggest the following deployment strategy. When a strong backbone such as iTransformer or FEDformer is available, the proposed framework can be used directly to obtain both competitive static forecasting accuracy and additional online gains under \auditbad{delayed feedback}{P03}. When computational or operational constraints are tighter, Stage~III alone already provides a \auditbad{robust and lightweight improvement}{P34} over the frozen baseline. When stronger online flexibility is allowed, enabling all three stages offers the best opportunity to exploit short-term drift, especially on backbones whose mechanism layer and relevance signal are sufficiently stable. This staged interpretation is important for practice because it allows the proposed method to be deployed under different \auditbad{latency, stability, and update-budget constraints}{P34} without changing the overall framework.

\section{Conclusion}
This paper proposes a plug-and-play online load forecasting framework that combines invariant learning, factor-based mechanism modeling, and a three-stage online controller. The experiments show that the framework is compatible with multiple transformer-based backbones, \auditbad{consistently improves over frozen deployment under shifted online streams}{P33}, and remains competitive against \auditbad{representative online forecasting baselines}{P20}. The empirical evidence further indicates that Stage~III provides the \auditbad{most reliable single-step gain}{P24}, while the joint controller yields the \auditbad{strongest average performance}{P25} when the online update policy is sufficiently constrained.
